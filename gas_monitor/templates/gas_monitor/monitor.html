Project thesis · what FlowTrace is testing Read the thesis +

FlowTrace measures a contradiction in Australia's east coast gas system: the country exports most of its gas from one coast while the far end of the same pipeline network struggles to keep Melbourne warm in winter. Built from the operator's own public data, it tracks where gas is produced, where it moves, and when the network is running against its limits.

Three things stand out so far. LNG export accounts for the large majority of all end-use gas — generation, the subject of most energy-transition commentary, is a fraction of it, though still enough to set a floor under electricity prices. The pipelines feeding Curtis Island have been flagged as threatened on nearly every day for months, a structural condition rather than a passing event. And stress is regional: storage in one state can sit near a record low while another sits comfortably above normal, a pattern a single national number would hide entirely.

The wider claim: whenever a domestic market is joined to a global one by an export terminal, the domestic remainder pays a cost worth measuring directly rather than assuming away. Prices are not yet built into FlowTrace, so its commercial half — what that cost actually is — remains the next piece of work.

Source: AEMO Gas Bulletin Board, fetched from nemweb. This thesis is the standard the rest of the design is held to.

Where the gas came from

Two demand measures, side by side and never added. End use counts facilities reporting directly and is blind where consumption arrives through a transmission system — Victoria's end-use figure understates its real load, because Melbourne is served by a pipeline rather than facilities that report demand directly. Delivered counts what pipelines put into the state, which catches that load but double counts wherever a pipeline feeds a facility that also reports. No net column: one balance would have to pick a side and be wrong for half the states.
